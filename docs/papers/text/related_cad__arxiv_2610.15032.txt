Abstract
Industrial anomaly detection benchmarks have advanced rapidly, yet they remain
largely static and therefore underrepresent deployment settings in which inspection
systems must be extended from one industrial component to another over time. In our
setting, continualindustrialanomalydetectionisframedasatask-incrementalproblem:
each component defines a task, while the learning objective remains semi-supervised.
The goal extends beyond conventional anomaly detection, since models should preserve
their performance on all components as they are sequentially exposed to new compo-
nents. This framing is especially relevant because many existing industrial pipelines
either assume fully supervised defect labels or study only isolated datasets, leaving
open the question of how semi-supervised anomaly detectors behave across a sequence
of heterogeneous components. We address this gap by organizing the benchmark as a
sequence of related component-level tasks and by defining multiple scenarios, includ-
ing random, easy-to-hard, and hard-to-easy. In addition to single-dataset continual
scenarios, we propose challenging scenarios spanning across multiple datasets. We
further evaluate continual performance from two complementary perspectives: image-
level anomaly detection and pixel-level anomaly localization. By recasting industrial
anomaly detection as a continual benchmark rather than a static train/test problem,
our work establishes a concrete path toward studying knowledge retention, adaptation,
and generalization in evolving industrial environments.
1 Introduction
Industrial anomaly detection has achieved strong results on standard benchmarks, yet most
published settings remain static: one model is trained offline on a fixed distribution and
then evaluated on a held-out split drawn from the same or closely related conditions. Real
deployment is significantly more challenging. New components are introduced over time,
and an inspection system that works well for one component often has to be extended to
additional ones without retraining from scratch on the full historical data. A practical
anomaly detector must therefore do more than generalize once; it must continually adapt
while preserving previously acquired knowledge.
This requirement exposes a gap between current industrial anomaly detection and con-
tinual learning. In static anomaly detection, the dominant objective is to separate normal
and anomalous patterns for one dataset at one time. In a continual setting considered here,

the objective changes: the model must absorb new component-specific normality patterns
without degrading its performance on previously learned components. The central drawback
is catastrophic forgetting, which is especially costly in industrial inspection because a sys-
tem that forgets how to model earlier components can miss anomalies when those products
reappear in production.
Theneedforcontinualanomalydetectionisreinforcedbythestructureofindustrialdata.
Indeed, in real-world settings, defective samples may be scarce, incomplete, or too costly
to annotate comprehensively. As a result, many real-world anomaly-detection problems are
inherently semi-supervised, in the sense that they do not assume availability of anomalies as
training data, they train models only on normal samples, and detect deviations at test time.
Under this normal-only setting, the continual problem is not to incrementally learn defect
classes, but to adapt the notion of normality across a sequence of components. Methods
that work well in a single static benchmark may still be brittle when they must preserve
normal representations for earlier components while learning new ones.
Motivated by this gap, we frame continual industrial anomaly detection as a benchmark
problem in which each task corresponds to one component and the inspection objective
remains fixed across the full sequence. The goal is then not just merely adaptation to multi-
ple labeled defect categories. Instead, the model has to maintain strong anomaly detection
on all components as it is sequentially exposed to new component distributions under a
normal-only training regime. This perspective also makes it possible to study benchmark
configurationsthathavereceivedlittleattentionincontinualanomalydetection: curriculum-
style orderings that explicitly control task difficulty, and multi-dataset streams that combine
component-level and dataset-level distribution shifts. In particular, we consider random or-
derings, easy-to-hard and hard-to-easy curricula, and multi-dataset scenarios. In addition,
we evaluate the continual setting from two complementary viewpoints: image-level anomaly
detection and pixel-level anomaly localization.
In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:
- We formulate continual industrial anomaly detection as a benchmark problem motivated
by realistic production and inspection workflows, where each component defines a task
challenging model adaptation and knowledge retention.
- We focus on the semi-supervised setting, where models are exclusively trained with normal
data, and the challenge is continual adaptation across components rather than incremental
learning of multiple supervised defect classes.
- We introduce principled benchmark scenarios that go beyond prior continual anomaly-
detection studies by explicitly evaluating curriculum-based orderings (easy-to-hard and
hard-to-easy) and multi-dataset streams, enabling a cohesive analysis across diverse datasets
and task sequences.
- We devise non-trivial continual adaptations of PatchCore, PaDiM, and CFA and per-
form an extensive evaluation that considers state-of-the-art continual industrial anomaly
detection models across two perspectives (image-level, pixel-level), providing a multi-faceted
analysis.
2 Related Works

by datasets such as MVTec AD, MPDD, and VisA, which stress different aspects of visual
defects, from structural surface anomalies to logical inconsistencies in object arrangement.
These benchmarks established strong baselines for industrial visual inspection, but they
usually assume a static train/test split and independent evaluation within each dataset.
ADBench [Han et al., 2022] is a broad anomaly-benchmarking work that systematizes eval-
uation across supervision regimes and dataset types, but it is not specific to industrial
visual inspection and does not study sequential learning. In industrial vision, CableInspect-
AD [Arodi et al., 2024] introduces a challenging expert-annotated benchmark for robotic

components arrive sequentially, models are trained only on normal samples, and evalua-
tion explicitly measures retention, forgetting, and adaptation under alternative task or-

search is also dominated by non-continual methods that assume a static deployment set-
ting. Reconstruction-based methods model the appearance manifold of normal samples and
flag anomalies through reconstruction mismatch or adversarially regularized feature space.
GANomaly is the representative reconstruction baseline in our experiments, combining an
encoder–decoder–encoder architecture with adversarial training for normality modeling [Ak-
cay et al., 2018]. More recent reconstruction and restoration variants strengthen this idea
with richer architectures and objectives, including semantic inpainting [Tao et al., 2022],
masked-patch reconstruction [Jiang et al., 2023], multilevel feature restoration [Guo et al.,
2024], and reconstruction regularization with memory or text guidance [Zhang et al., 2024,
Qiang et al., 2025].
Another major family relies on pretrained features, patchwise distributions, or memory-
based matching rather than explicit image reconstruction. PatchCore [Roth et al., 2022]
stores a coreset of representative normal patch embeddings and scores test patches by
nearest-neighbordistance, andPaDiM[Defardetal.,2021]fitsGaussianmodelstopatchwise
features at each spatial location. CFA [Lee et al., 2022] complements these memory- and
distribution-based approaches by learning compact normal feature representations through
a soft-boundary objective.
Teacher–student and distillation-based methods form a further relevant thread.
STFPM [Wang et al., 2021] aligns multiscale teacher and student feature pyramids so
that anomalies emerge where the student fails to reproduce normal teacher activations.
RD4AD [Deng and Li, 2022] reconstructs teacher embeddings from compressed one-class
representations, EfficientAD [Batzner et al., 2024] retains the teacher–student intuition
while targeting millisecond-level inference, and PaSTe [Barusco et al., 2025a] pushes this
direction toward edge deployment.
Finally, density estimation methods such as FastFlow [Yu et al., 2021] model the density
of normal samples using normalizing flows and provides a complementary probabilistic per-
spective to reconstruction, patch-memory, and teacher–student detectors. Recent work also
extends industrial anomaly detection toward multimodal inspection, more realistic static
benchmarks, and weakly supervised localization [Cheng et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025,
Sime et al., 2026].

However, several gaps remain directly relevant to our work. First, even methods trained
only on normal data are almost always evaluated in isolation on a single static benchmark.
Second, this literature rarely studies how image-level detection and pixel-level localization
behave together under sequential learning. Third, static evaluations do not measure reten-
tion after a model encounters new industrial concepts. As a result, the non-continual state
of the art provides strong static baselines but leaves open the task-incremental, normal-only,
multi-dataset setting that we target.
Continual learning for industrial inspection. Continual learning provides a natural
framework for industrial settings where data arrive in stages and the target distribution
evolves. Most continual learning benchmarks, however, focus on closed-set classification.
Industrial anomaly detection differs in three ways: it is anomaly-centric, it is often trained
only on normal samples, and it may require localization in addition to image-level decisions.
These properties motivate a benchmark in which anomaly sensitivity and forgetting control
are evaluated jointly. Direct continual anomaly-detection work is still rare, but the recent
literature already suggests several distinct continual-learning strategies. A first line focuses
on online updating and lightweight rehearsal. Gao et al. [2023] proposed LeMO, a fully
online anomaly-detection and localization method that replaces offline pre-collection with a
learnablememoryupdatedjointlywithfeatureextractionthroughacontrastiveobjective. Li
et al. [2022] explicitly introduced continual anomaly detection for industrial inspection and
proposed the distribution of normal embeddings, which summarizes previous tasks through
normal-feature distributions and leverages synthetic anomalies so that anomaly detection
can benefit from supervised continual-learning machinery. In a more deployment-oriented
direction, Barusco et al. [2025b] studied continual anomaly detection on edge devices, show-
ing that replay and compressed replay can make lightweight teacher–student models such
as STFPM and PaSTe practical under strict memory budgets.
Other studies pursue parameter-efficient adaptation or stronger generative mechanisms.
Liu et al. [2024] proposed UCAD, where a key–prompt–knowledge memory bank and
structure-based contrastive learning yield a prompt-based continual learner that separates
task-specific normal knowledge from task-invariant anomaly cues. Tang et al. [2024] in-
tegrated incremental learning into a unified reconstruction framework for small defect in-
spection, using object-aware self-attention and semantic compression to reduce feature con-
flicts without storing large feature banks. Diffusion-based methods have become especially
prominent in recent work. Li et al. [2025] introduced One-for-More, a continual diffusion
model that combines gradient projection, an iterative singular-value-decomposition scheme
for memory reduction, and anomaly-masked conditioning to limit forgetting and halluci-
nated reconstructions. Hu et al. [2025] then pushed replay further with ReplayCAD, where
a pretrained diffusion model is used for generative replay to improve especially anomaly
segmentation. Finally, continual anomaly detection is beginning to expand beyond 2D
RGB inspection: Lu et al. [2025] proposed C3D-AD for 3D anomaly detection, combining
kernel-attention feature normalization, a learnable advisor, and representation-preserving
parameter perturbation to handle emerging point-cloud categories. A task-aware parameter
decoupling framework proposed by Zhang et al. [2026] moves from one-model-per-category
assumptions to a one-for-more setting through instance-aware prompt tuning and gradient-
aware parameter decoupling.
The current continual literature remains much narrower than the broader non-continual
anomaly-detection landscape, but it already spans online memory learning, replay-based
and generative-replay strategies, prompt-based adaptation, and architectural mechanisms
for incremental reconstruction. However, important limitations remain. Several approaches
still incorporate supervision such as synthetic anomalies or explicit defect-oriented signals,
which weakens the match to purely normal-only industrial training. Moreover, existing
continual studies are typically limited to one dataset or one scenario at a time, so they do
not provide a cohesive analysis across diverse benchmarks, alternative task orderings, and
cross-dataset streams. This leaves curriculum effects and multi-dataset continual anomaly-

detection scenarios largely unexplored, even though both are central to understanding how
task difficulty and broader domain shift affect forgetting. Finally, the literature does not yet
systematically compare image-level and pixel-level continual anomaly detection within the
same benchmark design. These gaps are precisely what motivate the benchmark proposed
in this paper.
3 The InCLAD Benchmark
The proposed benchmark is defined within industrial visual inspection. Every task concerns
anomaly detection in manufactured products or industrial scenes, but each task corresponds
to a different inspected component or concept. In this task-incremental framing, distribution
shift is driven primarily by component-specific normality rather than by supervised defect-
class changes. This matches deployment settings in which inspection systems should absorb
new product variants incrementally without losing competence on previous components.
InCLAD is therefore a benchmark protocol rather than a raw-data collection effort:
it builds on public industrial datasets and contributes a continual learning layer of task
definitions, orderings, metrics, and evaluation artifacts over them.
Task Definition: We consider a stream of tasks T1, T2, . . . , TN . Each task contains
normal training samples, anomalous test samples (only in the test portion of the task), and,
when available, pixel-level masks for anomalous regions. The benchmark is evaluated from
two complementary perspectives: image-level anomaly detection, which asks whether an
image is abnormal, and pixel-level anomaly localization, which asks where the anomalous
region appears. The task identity is tied to the inspected component or concept rather
than to a defect label. Across a stream, shifts may arise from new products, materials,
or surface geometries; from different industrial datasets; from acquisition changes such as
illumination, camera configuration, viewpoint, resolution, or background; or from temporal
and batch evolution in production. The key property is that the inspection objective remains
anomaly detection while the definition of normality changes over time.
Single-Dataset Scenarios: We instantiate task streams through an empirical scenario-
extraction procedure based on single-task anomaly-detection difficulty. The first stage starts
from raw dataset concepts and converts each dataset into three continual-learning scenar-
ios. For each concept, we train Single-Task Experts independently using three representative
state-of-the-art methods: PatchCore [Roth et al., 2022], PaDiM [Defard et al., 2021], and
CFA [Lee et al., 2022], and collect concept-level ROC-AUC scores. This verifies that can-
didate tasks are learnable, with ROC-AUC values above the random-guessing level of 0.5,
and provides a task-wise difficulty estimate that is independent of any continual-learning
strategy. To avoid defining difficulty from a single detector, we rank concepts separately for
PatchCore, PaDiM, and CFA, then average the ranks across the three methods. These aver-
age ranks define the within-dataset curricula. The easy-to-hard sequence orders tasks from
easiest to hardest, the hard-to-easy sequence reverses this order, and the random sequence
provides a non-curriculum reference. This yields three within-dataset scenarios per dataset.
The same scenario definitions are used for image-level and pixel-level evaluation, enabling a
direct comparison between detection and localization under identical task streams.
Figure 2 visualizes the first-stage evidence used to derive these scenarios. In each
heatmap, rows correspond to concepts sorted by the consensus ordering induced by the
average rank, cell values report the ROC-AUC obtained by each Single-Task Expert, and
the final column reports the average ROC-AUC across PatchCore, PaDiM, and CFA. We use
these three methods because they are competitive industrial anomaly detectors with distinct
inductive biases: PatchCore uses memory-based nearest-neighbor retrieval, PaDiM models
location-aware patch distributions with Gaussian statistics, and CFA enforces compactness
of the normal feature space through hypersphere-based objectives. This reduces the risk
that the scenario definition is dominated by one detector family. The exact hard-to-easy
rankings and pairwise Spearman correlations are reported in Appendix E.

Multi-Dataset Scenarios: Beyond within-dataset streams, we define a multi-dataset
scenario in which the learner encounters tasks drawn from the full dataset pool. This setting
combines task-level changes with dataset-level distribution shifts, making it a stress test for
retention and adaptation under broader industrial variability. The multi-dataset stream is
constructed from a balanced subset of tasks selected according to the Single-Task Expert
difficulty estimates. For each dataset with more than six tasks, we retain six representative
concepts using the average ROC-AUC column in the difficulty heatmaps: the two easiest
tasks, the two hardest tasks, and two median-difficulty tasks. For datasets with six or
fewer tasks, we retain all available tasks. This rule preserves both extremes of the difficulty
spectrum while preventing the largest datasets from dominating the stream, and it yields a
27-task multi-dataset scenario. After task selection, we define two cross-dataset orderings:
easy-to-hard and hard-to-easy, obtained by sorting the retained tasks according to the same
average-difficulty criterion.

and updates under a continual-learning constraint, meaning unrestricted access to all past
data is not assumed. After training on Tt, the model is evaluated on the test sets of all tasks
seen so far, T1, . . . , Tt. The goal is to adapt to the current visual regime while retaining
anomaly-detection ability on earlier tasks. We report standard anomaly-detection metrics
for both perspectives: image-level ROC-AUC for detection and pixel-level ROC-AUC when
segmentation masks are available. Continual-learning behavior is summarized through final
average performance and backward transfer (BWT). In particular, our continual ROC-AUC
averages the ROC-AUC values obtained throughout the learner’s lifetime after each task

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
All experiments use the benchmark protocol defined above. We evaluate each method on
identical task streams, train/test splits, and ordering metadata, and compare naive non-
continual baselines against their continual variants at both image and pixel level. Unless
otherwise specified, continual versions use replay; PatchCore, PaDiM, and CFA require
method-specific continual adaptations because their original formulations do not directly
support replay-based updates. Detailed metric definitions, continual adaptations, execution-
time measurements, and hyperparameter settings are provided in Appendices B, C, D.3,
and D.
4.2 Industrial Datasets
We build our benchmark upon several public industrial datasets. DAGM [Weimer et al.,
2016] is a long-standing industrial inspection benchmark built around visually subtle de-
fects embedded in heavily textured backgrounds. Its value lies in separating weak defect
cues from repetitive texture, which is relevant when anomalies are small relative to the back-
ground structure. Following the protocol summarized in [Xu et al., 2025], we also consider
MVTec AD [Bergmann et al., 2019], and VisA [Zou et al., 2022]. These benchmarks are
complementary industrial anomaly-detection scenarios. MVTec AD contains 15 real-world
categories and is a standard benchmark for structural anomaly localization. VisA covers 12

object categories with both surface and structural defects under varying object configura-
tions. BTAD [Mishra et al., 2021] is a real-world industrial anomaly benchmark introduced
for manufacturing inspection and anomaly-detection research. It contains 2540 images of
three product categories, commonly denoted as product_1, product_2, and product_3, to-
gether with pixel-level annotations that can be interpreted as instance segmentation masks
and transformed into semantic segmentation or object-detection labels. Of the 2540 images,
2261 are unlabeled and 279 carry anomaly annotations, and the dataset can also be viewed
through an ok/ko split with 2250 normal and 290 anomalous images. BTAD is useful be-
cause it combines pixel-level localization with a semisupervised industrial setting in which
only a limited subset of images is annotated. MPDD [Jezek et al., 2021] is a dataset for
visual defect detection in industrial metal-parts manufacturing. It contains more than 1000
images with pixel-precise defect masks and is explicitly designed to benchmark methods
under realistic conditions for metal-part inspection. The dataset is divided into a training
subset containing anomaly-free samples and a validation subset containing both normal and
anomalous samples. MPDD is especially relevant here because it targets metallic industrial
components, making it closer in spirit to materials-oriented quality-control problems than
many consumer-object anomaly datasets.
Dataset-license details and compute resources are reported in Appendix D.
4.3 Anomaly Detection Methods
Our experimental comparison includes representative non-continual baselines from the main
method families reviewed in Related Works. Specifically, we consider nine methods: the
reconstruction-based baseline GANomaly [Akcay et al., 2018]; pretrained-feature and
patch-distribution methods PatchCore [Roth et al., 2022], PaDiM [Defard et al., 2021],
and CFA [Lee et al., 2022]; teacher–student and distillation methods STFPM [Wang
etal.,2021], RD4AD[DengandLi,2022], EfficientAD[Batzneretal.,2024], andPaSTe[Bar-
usco et al., 2025a]; and the flow-based baseline FastFlow [Yu et al., 2021]. This set of
methods is intended to cover the dominant non-continual design choices used in current
industrial anomaly detection while keeping the comparison manageable across datasets and
evaluation protocols.
For the continual versions, six of the nine methods are adapted with the standard replay
protocol: at each task, the model is updated using the current normal training samples
together with samples retained from previous tasks. In contrast, PatchCore, PaDiM, and
CFA require non-trivial algorithmic adaptations because their original formulations are not
conventional parametric learners to which replay can be directly applied. PatchCore stores
a coreset memory bank of normal patch embeddings; our continual version maintains task-
specific patch banks, stores the current task at the native coreset resolution, compresses
previous banks with a distance-weighted greedy coreset, and scores each test image against
the closest task bank. PaDiM fits location-aware Gaussian statistics rather than training
with gradient updates; our continual version therefore stores one Gaussian model per task
and selects, at inference time, the task distribution that yields the lowest image-level Maha-
lanobis anomaly score. CFA combines a trainable descriptor with a memory bank used by
its soft-boundary objective. For its continual version, we train the descriptor on the current
task plus replay samples, rebuild all per-task inference banks after each descriptor update so
that they remain in the same embedding space, and select the bank with the lowest anomaly
score for prediction. These adaptations allow the three memory- or statistics-based detec-
tors to participate in the same continual benchmark without collapsing their task-specific
normality models into a single mixed distribution. Additional notation and pseudocode for
these adaptations are provided in Appendix C.

4.4 Results Discussion
Table 2 summarizes the benchmark at the dataset level. The single-dataset rows average
over all 9 anomaly-detection models and the two backbones, whereas the MultiDataset
rows average over the three strongest methods selected from the single-dataset study: CFA,
PaSTe, and PaDiM. Across within-dataset streams, continual learning consistently improves
both detection quality and retention. At the image level, the average ROC-AUC increases
from 0.588 for NCL to 0.737 for CL, while BWT improves from −0.088 to −0.027. At the
pixel level, the same pattern is observed: ROC-AUC increases from 0.718 to 0.860 and BWT
improves from −0.074 to −0.010. Thus, the continual variants do not merely increase final
detection performance; they also reduce forgetting.
Effect of task ordering: Task ordering affects image-level performance more strongly
than pixel-level performance. Easy-to-Hard gives the highest average CL ROC-AUC at the
image level (0.764), followed by Random (0.738) and Hard-to-Easy (0.708). This suggests
9

===== PAGE 10 =====
that starting from easier concepts can stabilize image-level anomaly scoring before harder
conceptsareintroduced, whereasbeginningwithharderconceptsleadstoweakeradaptation.
Pixel-level CL ROC-AUC is more stable across orderings, ranging from 0.849 to 0.870,
indicating that localization quality is less sensitive to the curriculum direction once replay
or method-specific continual mechanisms are used. BWT follows the same trend: continual
learning makes forgetting consistently less negative for all orderings, with especially large
gains under Easy-to-Hard.
Adaptation versus retention: At the aggregate level of Table 2, the CL variants
provide the best adaptation–retention balance. They improve ROC-AUC on every dataset
and ordering while also moving BWT closer to zero, which indicates less interference with
previously learned tasks. The effect is most pronounced on BTAD, where image-level BWT
improves from −0.289 to −0.046 under Easy-to-Hard and pixel-level BWT improves from
−0.247 to −0.010. MPDD remains challenging at the image level, with lower average CL
ROC-AUC and more negative BWT than most datasets, but its pixel-level results remain
strong. This gap suggests that some anomalies are easier to localize than to classify at
the image level. Since the single-dataset rows average over all 9 methods, they support
conclusions about the CL protocol as a class; method-specific rankings require the per-
method breakdowns rather than this pooled summary.
Multi-dataset streams: The MultiDataset rows in Table 2 show that cross-dataset
streams are substantially more challenging for naive image-level detection, with NCL ROC-
AUC near chance under both Easy-to-Hard and Hard-to-Easy orderings. Continual learning
recovers much of this gap, improving image-level ROC-AUC to roughly 0.79 and pixel-
level ROC-AUC above 0.90 while keeping BWT close to zero. This suggests that the top-
performing continual methods can retain useful normality models even when task transi-
tions cross dataset boundaries, although the multi-dataset setting remains a stronger stress
test than most within-dataset streams. Appendix F provides the corresponding transition
heatmaps and a finer-grained analysis of cross-dataset interference.
The same appendix also complements the aggregate table for single-dataset streams. The
heatmaps expose the per-task trajectory after each update, making it possible to inspect
when forgetting occurs, whether it is localized to specific concepts, and how NCL and CL
differ under the same ordering. They therefore provide the fine-grained evidence needed
to interpret results beyond dataset-level averages. The key takeaway is that CL primarily
improves retained-task performance, especially in off-diagonal heatmap cells, and this effect
is more uniform for pixel-level localization.
5 Conclusion
This paper presents a benchmark for continual industrial anomaly detection under evolv-
ing production conditions. Rather than treating industrial inspection as a static train/test
problem, we cast it as a sequential setting in which models must retain anomaly-detection
ability on previously seen components while adapting to new products, acquisition condi-
tions, and dataset-level shifts. Through a large-scale evaluation across multiple industrial
datasets, task orderings, anomaly-detection perspectives, and continual-learning strategies,
the benchmark exposes how performance depends not only on the detector architecture,
but also on the structure of the task stream. This framing makes catastrophic forgetting,
backward transfer, and cross-dataset adaptation central evaluation targets.
Limitations: Although the benchmark covers diverse datasets and ordering regimes, it
remains limited by the scope of existing public industrial anomaly datasets, which only
partially reflect the temporal, operational, and sensor-level variability of real production
lines. The proposed tasks approximate continual deployment by sequencing components and
datasets, but they do not capture every practical constraint, such as changing inspection
hardware, evolving quality standards, or long-term label drift. Future work should extend
the benchmark with richer temporal data, stronger cross-factory distribution shifts, and
10

===== PAGE 11 =====
standardized compute and memory budgets.
Broader Impacts: Continual industrial anomaly detection can have positive practical im-
pactbymakingvisualinspectionsystemsmoreadaptabletochangingproductionconditions.
Better retention across components, products, and acquisition settings may reduce unnec-
essary retraining, improve quality control, and support earlier detection of manufacturing
defects. In safety- or reliability-sensitive industrial domains, more robust anomaly detection
can also help reduce waste and limit downstream failures caused by defective parts. At the
same time, benchmark progress should not be interpreted as a guarantee of deployment
readiness. Incorrect anomaly scores may lead to missed defects, false alarms, unnecessary
product rejection, or misplaced trust in automated inspection. Continual systems can also
accumulate errors over time if changes in production are not monitored carefully. These
risks motivate human-in-the-loop validation, periodic auditing on recent production data,
clear reporting of failure cases, and deployment-specific checks before using such models in
operational decision-making.

B Benchmark Metrics
Continual-learning metrics should characterize more than performance on the most recent
task. Following prior work on lifelong-learning evaluation Lopez-Paz and Ranzato [2017],
Díaz-Rodríguez et al. [2018], we evaluate whether a method can learn incoming tasks, re-
tain knowledge of previously learned tasks, and transfer knowledge across the stream while
operating under the memory constraints imposed by the benchmark protocol.
Let T1, . . . , TN denote a task stream. During evaluation, we construct a results matrix

evaluation: in the image-level case, Ri,j stores image-level ROC-AUC; in the pixel-level
case, it stores pixel-level ROC-AUC when segmentation masks are available.

C.3 Continual CFA
CFA differs from PatchCore and PaDiM because it contains a trainable descriptor network
and a memory bank used by the CFA soft-boundary objective. The memory bank used
for training must remain compatible with the descriptor being updated, and old inference
banks become stale after every descriptor update. Our continual CFA therefore separates
the training bank from the inference banks.
Let gθ be the CFA descriptor. At task t, let Rt denote replay samples from previous
tasks and define Ut = Xt ∪ Rt. Before descriptor training, we initialize a training bank
Btrain t = Bank(gθt−1 (Ut)),
which serves as the fixed memory target for the CFA soft-boundary loss. We then update
the descriptor parameters by minimizing the standard CFA training loss on Ut:
θt = arg min θ LCFA(gθ (Ut), Btrain t ).
After the descriptor update, all per-task inference banks are rebuilt using the current de-
scriptor so that every bank lies in the same embedding space:
Binf j = Bank(gθt (Xj )), j = 1, . . . , t.

CFA CL update.
1. Store the current task samples Xt for future bank reconstruction.
2. Form Ut = Xt ∪ Rt from current data and replay samples.
3. Initialize the training bank Btrain t from gθt−1 (Ut).
4. Train the CFA descriptor on Ut with the fixed training bank.
5. Rebuild every per-task inference bank Binf 1 , . . . , Binf t using the updated descriptor gθt .
6. Calibrate the threshold on Xt and update the replay buffer.

E Scenario Ordering Details
This appendix reports the exact concept orderings used to derive the structured continual
scenarios in the first stage of the study (see Tables 8–13). The ordering tables serve two
purposes. First, they make the scenario construction reproducible by listing the hard-to-
easy sequence used for each dataset; the easy-to-hard curriculum is obtained by reversing
the same sequence. Second, they show how stable the notion of task difficulty is across
detector families. Table 8 reports the pairwise Spearman correlations among PatchCore,
PaDiM, and CFA. High correlations indicate that the three Single-Task Experts agree on
which concepts are easier or harder, while low or negative correlations indicate that difficulty
is partly method-dependent. This is why the benchmark uses average rank rather than the
score of a single detector to define curriculum orderings.
The agreement patterns differ substantially across datasets. BTAD has only three con-
cepts, but the models consistently identify product 02 as the hardest and product 03 as the
easiest. VisA also shows strong agreement across all detector pairs, with capsules and mac-
aroni variants near the hard end and chewinggum or pcb4 near the easy end. DAGM has
generally high agreement between PatchCore and PaDiM, but weaker agreement involving
CFA, showing that some synthetic texture classes favor different modeling biases. MVTec
AD occupies an intermediate regime: the easiest concepts such as bottle, leather, and wood
are consistently strong, while concepts such as pill, screw, and capsule form the hard end of
the stream. MPDD is the clearest case where ordering cannot be inferred reliably from one
detector alone. PaDiM and CFA are negatively correlated on MPDD, and concepts such as
metal_plate are easy for CFA but difficult for PaDiM. The average-rank ordering therefore
prevents the curriculum from being dominated by one method’s failure mode.
These differences matter for interpreting continual-learning results. Datasets with strong
agreement, such as VisA and BTAD, provide curricula where task difficulty is relatively
stable across model families. Datasets with weaker agreement, especially MPDD and parts
of MVTec AD, create more ambiguous streams: a task may be easy for memory-based
retrieval but difficult for Gaussian statistics, or vice versa. Such streams are useful stress
tests because forgetting and backward transfer are then affected not only by nominal task
order, but also by how well a continual method’s inductive bias matches each concept.

F Fine-grained Heatmap Results
Figures 3–12 provide the task-transition heatmaps used to complement the aggregate re-
sults in Table 2. Each triangular block corresponds to one ordering. Diagonal cells show
performance when a task is first learned, whereas off-diagonal cells show whether that task
is retained after subsequent updates. Across datasets, the main pattern is that NCL often
preserves strong diagonal performance but loses intensity in off-diagonal cells. CL produces
warmer and more uniform lower-triangular regions, indicating that the gains in the aggre-
gate table are driven not only by better final scores but also by better retention of individual
tasks.
Ordering effects. Ordering effects are most visible in the off-diagonal cells. In NCL, the
same task can perform well immediately after training and then degrade sharply after later
tasksareintroduced. ThisisespeciallyvisibleinBTADandMPDDattheimagelevel, where
several early-task cells become much darker after subsequent updates. Random streams
usually fall between the two curriculum directions, confirming that the benchmark captures
the interaction between task sequence and the update rule rather than only average dataset
difficulty. CL reduces this ordering sensitivity: the color contrast between hard-to-easy,
easy-to-hard, and random blocks becomes less severe, particularly for pixel-level evaluation.
Thus, the heatmaps refine the aggregate result by showing that ordering effects are often
task-local, with a small number of vulnerable concepts driving much of the forgetting signal.
Adaptation versus retention. The NCL–CL contrast is strongest on retained-task en-
tries rather than on newly learned diagonal entries. BTAD provides the clearest example:
NCL includes high diagonal values, but retained performance on earlier tasks drops sub-
stantially; CL raises many of those off-diagonal cells and makes the three ordering blocks
more homogeneous. MPDD shows a related but more nuanced pattern. Image-level CL
improves retention relative to NCL, but the heatmaps remain less uniform than their pixel-
level counterparts, matching the main-paper observation that MPDD is harder for image-
level detection than for localization. MVTec and VisA also show reduced contrast between
newly learned and previously learned tasks under CL, although some low-intensity regions
remain. DAGM exhibits more moderate gains and retains class-dependent variability, sug-
gesting that continual adaptation reduces but does not eliminate concept-specific interfer-
ence. Across datasets, pixel-level heatmaps are generally warmer and more stable than
image-level heatmaps. This is particularly evident for MPDD, MVTec, and VisA, where CL
produces high and relatively uniform localization scores across many task transitions. This
separates two effects that are conflated in a single aggregate score: a model may preserve
local defect evidence well while still struggling to convert that evidence into robust image-
level anomaly scores. The heatmaps therefore explain why the main table can show strong
pixel-level CL performance even when image-level BWT remains more negative for some
datasets.
Multi-dataset streams. Figures 13–20 and 21–28 extend the transition-matrix analy-
sis to the 27-task multi-dataset stream for ResNet-18 and Wide ResNet-50-2, respectively.
These heatmaps are averaged across the three strongest methods selected from the single-
dataset study, CFA, PaDiM, and PaSTe, so they summarize behavior across multiple de-
tector families rather than a single model. Compared with the within-dataset matrices, the
multi-dataset heatmaps expose broader domain-shift effects: low-intensity bands are less
tied to a single product category and more often appear around transitions between source
datasets. This pattern is visible for both backbones, which indicates that the multi-dataset
scenario introduces interference beyond ordinary within-dataset task ordering rather than
an artifact of a particular feature extractor. The NCL–CL contrast remains clear in this
harder setting. For both Hard-to-Easy and Easy-to-Hard streams, Figures 13 and 19 show
